You might turn down an offer of dinner because you don’t want to put anyone out. Then evening comes, you haven’t eaten, and asking again feels awkward.

You may know you could use help and still feel uncomfortable asking for it. You might worry about inconveniencing someone, or feel that accepting help means admitting you cannot cope, especially when you wanted this baby so much.

## What makes asking feel difficult?

If you have usually been the dependable person in your family, needing care yourself may feel unfamiliar. You might be used to solving problems quietly or waiting until you have tried everything before involving anyone else.

You might put off asking your partner to take over until you are exhausted. Even an ordinary need, such as taking a shower, can feel too small to mention.

If previous requests brought criticism, unwanted advice, or reminders of how much someone had done for you, you may hesitate to ask again. Consider who you would feel comfortable calling, even with dishes in the sink and no energy to make conversation.

## Make the request smaller and clearer

“Let me know if you need anything” can be hard to answer when you’re exhausted. If you know what would help, name a task and a time. If you don’t, you could say, “I’m too tired to work out where to start. Could you suggest something you could take care of today?”

You could say, “Could you bring dinner on Tuesday? We’re keeping visits short, so a drop-off would be really helpful.”

Or, “Could you handle groceries this week, including checking what we need and putting everything away?”

Sometimes you want company: “Could you come over this afternoon? I’m finding the days on my own hard.”

You can make a request before things become unbearable. If the person cannot help, ask whether another time would work. You do not need to apologize for having asked.

## When your partner waits for instructions

If you parent with a partner, caring for the baby and running the household are shared responsibilities. When you have to explain every task and remind them again later, you are still organizing the work. Talk about who will keep track of a task as well as who will do it.

For example, agreeing that one person handles laundry means deciding together what that includes, then letting that person keep track of it. The same conversation can apply to meals or household supplies. You can revisit the arrangement as recovery, feeding, work, and sleep needs change.

## You can set limits around help

You might want someone you trust to care for the baby while you rest. Or you might prefer to stay with your baby while someone makes lunch. You can say which would help today, and change your mind tomorrow.

Visits can be welcome, too. You may enjoy seeing a friend and having an ordinary conversation. But if a visit means cleaning beforehand and serving coffee while you recover, you can ask for something simpler: “I’d love to see you. Could you bring lunch? I’m not up to hosting.” If feeding advice is wearing you down, try: “I’d like some company, but I don’t want to discuss feeding choices today.”

If someone will care for your baby, choose a person you trust who can follow the baby’s care and safety needs. You do not have to accept an arrangement that concerns you simply because it is offered as help.

## What if guilt is still there?

You may ask for something reasonable and still feel guilty afterward. Notice what you are telling yourself: “I should be able to do this alone,” or “I can rest once everything is finished.” Would you expect the same of a friend who had just given birth?

Self-compassion includes taking your own needs seriously when you’re struggling. If you catch yourself calling yourself lazy for needing a break, try describing what is actually happening: “I’ve been caring for the baby all morning and haven’t eaten. I need someone to take over while I have lunch.”

Even after a much-wanted pregnancy, you may find parts of caring for a newborn difficult. You can say so when you ask for support.

## When there is no obvious person to ask

Family may live far away, friends may be at work, and paid help may be out of reach. You may already have asked and found that no one is available. A clearer request cannot fix those circumstances.

Tell your healthcare team what you are managing without support and ask about local services or parent groups. A group might offer company, but it cannot necessarily cover childcare or give you time to sleep. Explain the practical problem you need help with, even if you do not know what the solution could be.

## When to talk with a professional

Talk with a healthcare professional if guilt, low mood, anxiety, or trouble sleeping even when you have the chance continues or makes daily life difficult. These experiences deserve assessment; this article cannot tell you whether you have postpartum depression or anxiety. You do not need to wait until you are at a breaking point to ask for an appointment.

You can learn about [postpartum therapy at Center for PTA](https://centerforpta.com/postpartum-therapy/) or [contact the practice](https://centerforpta.com/contact/) to discuss availability and whether the service fits your needs.

This article is educational and does not replace individual care. In the United States, call or text 988 for immediate crisis support. If you or your baby are in immediate danger, or you are losing touch with reality, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. Outside the United States, contact your local emergency service.
