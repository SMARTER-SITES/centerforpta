Dr. Jelena Djurovic, Psy.D.

You may have wanted this baby for a long time. You may love your baby and still dread another night, feel angry at small interruptions, or wonder where the person you used to be has gone. When other people ask how the baby is doing, it can be difficult to explain how you are doing.

I encourage you to talk about how you are doing. You do not need a perfect description of your feelings before asking for support. “I am having a harder time than I expected” is enough to begin a conversation.

Postpartum depression can affect mood, energy, concentration, sleep, and daily functioning. Anxiety may also need attention, whether or not depression is present. A professional assessment can help clarify what is happening and what care would fit your needs. [NIMH: Perinatal Depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression)

**Get urgent help now if you may act on thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, cannot keep either of you safe, or are confused or disconnected from reality. In the United States, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. Do not wait for a routine therapy appointment.** [NICHD action plan](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/ncmhep/initiatives/moms-mental-health-matters/moms/action-plan)

## Recognizing symptoms and asking for help

- Depression after birth can include anger, emptiness, or disconnection, as well as sadness.

- Symptoms that persist, worsen, or interfere with daily life deserve assessment. Severe symptoms deserve attention immediately.

- A screening questionnaire begins a conversation; it cannot establish a diagnosis by itself.

- Therapy, medical care when indicated, and practical support can all have a place in a care plan.

- You can ask for help while you are still functioning. You do not need to wait until you are unable to cope.

## Baby blues and postpartum depression: what is the difference?

Mild mood changes, tearfulness, and irritability can occur in the first days after birth. These “baby blues” usually settle within about two weeks. If symptoms continue, become more intense, or make it difficult to cope, contact a health care professional. The two-week distinction is not a reason to wait when you are struggling. [NHS: Postnatal depression](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/postnatal-depression/)

Postpartum depression is depression occurring after childbirth. It can begin in the weeks or months after birth. A difficult period that starts later still deserves attention. [NIMH: Perinatal Depression](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/perinatal-depression)

A CDC study following women in seven states identified depressive symptoms at nine to ten months postpartum, including among women who had not reported them earlier. It measured symptoms through survey questions, rather than diagnosing every participant. This is a reason to keep discussing mental health beyond the early postpartum visit. [Robbins and colleagues: Timing of Postpartum Depressive Symptoms](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2023/23_0107.htm)

## What if I feel angry or numb, rather than sad?

Depression does not always match the picture you have in mind. CDC guidance includes anger, feeling distant from the baby, crying, and doubts about being able to care for the baby. Persistent emptiness, loss of interest, or depleted energy may also warrant a conversation. [CDC: Symptoms of Depression Among Women](https://www.cdc.gov/reproductive-health/depression/index.html)

If your irritability frightens you, try to describe it without blaming yourself. When does it happen? How intense does it become? What happens afterward? Can you recover, or does the feeling stay with you? These details can be more useful in an assessment than deciding whether the word “depressed” feels right.

Anger alone is not enough to identify a mental health condition. It may also point to a need for rest, a more workable division of care, or a boundary that has been repeatedly ignored. An assessment should consider your symptoms and the demands of daily life.

## Recognizing postpartum anxiety

Anxiety after birth may involve worry that feels difficult to control, panic, bodily tension, trouble sleeping, or repeated checking. “Postpartum anxiety” can describe more than one anxiety condition. Depression and anxiety can overlap, but they should each be assessed rather than assumed to be the same problem. [ACOG: Summary of Perinatal Mental Health Conditions](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/summary-of-perinatal-mental-health-conditions)

Consider how often fear affects what you do during the day. Perhaps you cannot rest when another trusted adult is caring for the baby, or searching for reassurance keeps taking you back to the same worry. Bring those examples to a clinician. You can describe its effect on your day without first deciding whether it is irrational.

New or severe physical symptoms should also receive medical attention. Do not assume a physical problem is anxiety simply because you are anxious.

## Unwanted intrusive thoughts: how to talk about something frightening

An intrusive thought is an unwanted thought, image, or impulse that appears without invitation. During the perinatal period, some parents experience repeated frightening thoughts and respond with checking, avoidance, or rituals. When these experiences are distressing or interfere with daily life, discuss them with a qualified clinician. They may need an assessment for OCD or another condition. [International OCD Foundation: What is Perinatal OCD?](https://iocdf.org/perinatal-ocd/what-is-perinatal-ocd/)

A thought that horrifies you is different from wanting or planning to act on it. In one observational study, unwanted infant-harm thoughts were not associated with more reported aggression toward the infant. That finding cannot establish any individual's safety. A clinician still needs to assess the nature of the thoughts, intent, behavior, and other symptoms. [Fairbrother and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35235718/)

You might begin: “I am having thoughts I do not want. They scare me, and I need help explaining them.” If you want to act, have a plan, feel you could lose control, or cannot keep yourself or the baby safe, seek emergency help immediately.

## Why a professional assessment matters

Postpartum mental health difficulties have more than one possible explanation. A clinician may ask about when symptoms began, their intensity, your mental health history, sleep, current support, medications, and safety. Tell them about any past periods of unusually elevated mood or energy, markedly reduced need for sleep, or a diagnosis of bipolar disorder. This history can affect treatment decisions. [ACOG: Screening and Diagnosis, Clinical Practice Guideline 4](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2023/06/screening-and-diagnosis-of-mental-health-conditions-during-pregnancy-and-postpartum)

Your medical care matters too. For example, low thyroid hormone levels can produce depressive symptoms after birth. A medical professional can decide whether an examination or testing is appropriate. [Office on Women's Health: Postpartum depression](https://womenshealth.gov/mental-health/mental-health-conditions/postpartum-depression)

If your birth was frightening, tell the clinician what continues to trouble you: memories, avoidance, feeling on edge, or difficulty feeling safe. Trauma-related symptoms also deserve assessment. [ACOG: Summary of Perinatal Mental Health Conditions](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/summary-of-perinatal-mental-health-conditions)

You may be offered a questionnaire such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. ACOG recommends validated screening for depression and anxiety during pregnancy and postpartum, with access to assessment, treatment, and follow-up. Be honest about how you have been feeling. A screening result needs to be interpreted in context; follow-up should remain available even if a score does not capture your concern. [ACOG: Patient Screening](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/patient-screening)

## When the baby arrived after infertility or pregnancy loss

If becoming a parent followed infertility, treatment, or loss, you may feel pressure to be grateful every moment. You may think, “I worked so hard for this. How can I be struggling now?”

That question can make it harder to speak honestly. You can be grateful for your baby and need relief from the demands of caring for your baby. Your earlier longing does not remove your need for sleep, company, medical care, or emotional support.

Tell the clinician about the history that matters to you. There is no need to force every feeling into a single explanation. The related article on [pregnancy after infertility or loss](https://centerforpta.com/blog/pregnancy-after-infertility-or-loss/) explores the uncertainty and grief that can accompany that earlier stage.

## Identity, guilt, and the pressure to look as though you are coping

You may miss privacy, work, intimacy, or the ability to leave the house without planning around someone else's needs. Talking about what you miss can help you describe how life has changed. You can miss these things and still love your child.

Try making one concern more specific. Instead of “I should be better at this,” you might say, “I need an uninterrupted meal,” “I miss talking about something other than feeding,” or “I am afraid to tell people that I am not enjoying this week.”

Specific requests help others understand what you need. If guilt, hopelessness, or disconnection keep returning despite the support you can arrange, include that in your assessment. Practical difficulties and a mental health condition can require attention at the same time.

## Treatment options and the evidence

A care plan should reflect symptoms, safety, history, preferences, and access to care. A federal AHRQ review found moderate evidence that CBT and interpersonal therapy reduce depressive symptoms compared with usual care in perinatal depressive disorders. Evidence was more limited for several other conditions and for direct comparisons with medication. No single finding tells us which treatment will fit every person. [AHRQ: Nonpharmacologic Treatments for Maternal Mental Health Conditions](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/products/mental-health-pregnant/research)

**Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** works with patterns of thinking and behavior. A review of 31 studies found improvement in perinatal depression, while noting substantial differences between studies and concerns about study quality. [Pettman and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36991389/)

**Interpersonal therapy (IPT)** focuses on relationships, support, communication, and changes in roles. An early randomized trial of 120 women found improvements in depression and social adjustment compared with a waiting list. It did not establish a universal number of sessions or show that IPT is best for everyone. [O'Hara and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11074869/)

A newer review of 78 randomized trials supported CBT for perinatal depression and anxiety, with moderate confidence in those findings; confidence was lower for some other approaches. [Leng and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42343881/)

Medication may also be appropriate. Discuss benefits, risks, breastfeeding, previous responses, and follow-up with a qualified prescribing clinician. ACOG advises against withholding or stopping mental health medication solely because of pregnancy or lactation. Do not start, stop, or change medication on your own. [ACOG: Assessment and Treatment](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/assessment-and-treatment-of-perinatal-mental-health-conditions)

At Center for PTA, my role is psychological assessment and psychotherapy. Medication and postpartum medical concerns require the appropriate medical professional. Ask how care can be coordinated, with your permission, when more than one clinician is involved.

## Self-compassion in difficult moments

Self-compassion can begin with the way you respond to a hard moment. You might say: “I am overwhelmed right now. What would make the next hour more manageable?” Then choose a concrete action: contact someone, postpone a nonessential task, or explain what help you need.

A trial of the online “Be a Mom” program found improvements in symptoms and self-compassion among women at increased risk of postpartum depression. The program included several psychological components. It does not prove that self-compassion alone treats postpartum depression, or that a brief exercise replaces care. [Carona and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36930182/)

If that way of talking to yourself feels awkward, keep it simple: “I have had very little rest, and this is difficult.” You do not need to force yourself to feel positive. You can discuss self-criticism and shame in [self-compassion-focused therapy](https://centerforpta.com/self-compassion-therapy/) while receiving the care your symptoms require.

## How to ask for support

You do not need to complete all of these steps today. Start with the one you can do.

- **Tell someone you trust.** “I am not feeling like myself. I need help arranging a conversation with a clinician.” If the person closest to you is not supportive, choose someone else or contact a professional directly.

- **Contact your obstetric clinician, primary care clinician, or a mental health professional.** Describe what has changed, how long it has lasted, and how it affects your day. Say explicitly if you cannot sleep even when you have an opportunity, or if you are worried about safety.

- **Write down a few examples.** A short note about mood, worry, sleep, and difficult moments may make the appointment easier. A brief note is enough; you do not have to keep a detailed diary.

- **Ask for help with a specific task.** Ask for a meal, a ride, appointment time, or someone to take responsibility for a household task. Plan rest and feeding support with the appropriate clinicians when needed.

- **Ask what happens next.** Who will follow up? What should you do if symptoms worsen before the next appointment? If cost, insurance, transportation, childcare, or privacy is a barrier, bring that into the discussion.

If you have a history of depression or significant risk factors, you can also discuss a support plan during pregnancy. The USPSTF recommends counseling for people at increased risk who do not currently have depression. That prevention recommendation is separate from treatment for an existing condition. [USPSTF: Perinatal Depression, Preventive Interventions](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/perinatal-depression-preventive-interventions)

## For partners, family, and friends

Ask about the parent as well as the baby. Give them time to answer honestly. You might say: “I am concerned about how hard this has become. Would you like me to sit with you while you make the call?”

Offer a task you can actually carry through: arrange transportation, prepare food, handle a chore, or help protect appointment time. Let the parent say what kind of company or privacy they need. Depression and anxiety still require appropriate professional care; practical help can support access to that care. [NICHD action plan](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/ncmhep/initiatives/moms-mental-health-matters/moms/action-plan)

If you notice confusion, unusual beliefs, hallucinations, or a sudden major change in behavior, act promptly. Someone experiencing psychosis may not recognize that they need help. [NHS: Postpartum psychosis](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-partum-psychosis/)

## When help is urgent

Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. Warning signs can include hallucinations, beliefs disconnected from reality, severe confusion, or unusually elevated or agitated behavior with major sleep changes. It requires urgent medical assessment. [NHS: Postpartum psychosis](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-partum-psychosis/)

- **Immediate danger, possible action on harm thoughts, or suspected psychosis:** in the United States, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. Ask a trusted adult to stay with you and help keep the baby safe.

- **Suicidal thoughts or a mental health crisis:** call or text **988**, or use the [988 Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/). It is available around the clock. Immediate danger needs emergency care.

- **Support and help finding resources during or after pregnancy:** call or text **1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262)**, the free, confidential, 24/7 [National Maternal Mental Health Hotline](https://mchb.hrsa.gov/programs-impact/national-maternal-mental-health-hotline). It supports parents and families and can help connect you with care. It does not replace emergency services.

These numbers are for the United States. If you are elsewhere, use local emergency and crisis services. A routine practice contact form is not an emergency service.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I love my baby and still need help?

Yes. You can love your baby and still feel overwhelmed or unwell. Explain how you feel and what has become difficult, including any guilt about asking for help.

### Do I have to wait two weeks before calling someone?

No. Ask for support when you are concerned. Persistent symptoms deserve assessment, and severe symptoms or a safety concern require prompt attention. The baby-blues timeframe is not a waiting requirement.

### Can symptoms start several months after birth?

Yes. Continue discussing mental health throughout the first postpartum year. Symptoms beginning later are worth bringing to a clinician, even if an earlier appointment or screening raised no concerns.

### Will a questionnaire tell me whether I have postpartum depression?

It can identify concerns to discuss. A diagnosis requires a clinical assessment that considers symptoms, history, functioning, safety, and possible alternative explanations.

### Is therapy available if I am breastfeeding?

Yes. Breastfeeding does not prevent someone from receiving psychotherapy. If medication is also being considered, discuss your feeding situation and the individual benefits and risks with the prescribing clinician.

### What should I say in the first appointment?

“Since the birth, I have been feeling \_\_\_. It has been going on for \_\_\_. The hardest part of my day is \_\_\_. I need help understanding what is happening.” Include concerns about sleep, intrusive thoughts, or safety rather than trying to make the description sound less serious.
