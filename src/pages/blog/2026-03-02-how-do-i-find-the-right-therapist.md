Finding the right therapist matters. That does not mean there is one perfect therapist waiting for every person, or that a warm first impression is enough by itself. A good fit combines appropriate credentials and clinical competence with a working relationship in which you can ask questions, understand the plan, and speak honestly about what is or is not helping.

Research has consistently found an association between the therapeutic alliance and psychotherapy outcomes. A large [meta-analysis of 295 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29792475/) found a reliable positive relationship between alliance and outcome across treatment approaches, client characteristics, and countries. This does not mean that relationship is the only factor. The therapist's competence, the suitability of the treatment, your needs and preferences, practical access, and many other factors also matter.

So how do you make a thoughtful choice without expecting yourself to know everything before the first session?

## 1. Verify the professional license

Start with the basic safety question: is the clinician licensed, in good standing, and authorized to provide the service where you will physically be located?

In Illinois, you can search the [Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation license lookup](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/checklicense.html). A practice website, insurance directory, Psychology Today profile, or referral can help you find someone, but the official state record is the stronger source for license verification.

Professional titles can refer to different training paths and scopes of practice. Ask what license the clinician holds and whether it is appropriate for the service you need.

## 2. Ask about experience with your concern

A therapist does not need to have lived your exact experience, but should be able to explain their relevant training and experience. You might ask:

- Do you regularly work with this type of concern?
- What would you want to understand before deciding whether we are a good fit?
- Are there situations in which you would recommend another specialist?
- How do culture, language, identity, relationships, or medical circumstances shape your approach?

These questions are especially important when you are seeking a specialized service such as reproductive mental health support, trauma therapy, couples work, immigration psychological evaluation, or pre-surgical assessment.

## 3. Ask how the therapist works

You do not need to know every therapy acronym. The therapist should be able to explain the approach in everyday language.

Ask what early sessions usually involve, how goals are established, what you may do between sessions, and how progress is reviewed. The [American Psychological Association's psychotherapy guide](https://www.apa.org/topics/psychotherapy/understanding) describes effective psychotherapy as a collaboration that combines an appropriate evidence-based approach, clinical expertise, and the client's characteristics, culture, values, and preferences.

A clear answer does not have to be rigid. Good treatment plans can change as the therapist learns more, but you should understand the overall direction of the work.

## 4. Notice how it feels to ask questions

You do not have to feel completely comfortable in the first conversation. Starting therapy can be unfamiliar, and some topics are difficult by nature. Still, notice whether the therapist listens, answers questions respectfully, explains boundaries, and makes room for your perspective.

Fit is not the same as always agreeing. A therapist may challenge you, notice something uncomfortable, or suggest a direction you had not considered. The important question is whether the work feels collaborative rather than coercive, dismissive, or confusing.

## 5. Discuss language, culture, and identity directly

Language can shape how easily a person describes emotions, family history, migration, grief, trauma, or relationship patterns. You can ask whether the therapist provides the service in your preferred language and how they approach cultural context.

## 6. Confirm the practical details

Even a strong clinical fit may not be sustainable if the logistics do not work. Ask about:

- current availability and appointment frequency;
- in-person, telehealth, or hybrid options;
- fees, accepted insurance, and expected out-of-pocket costs;
- cancellation and missed-appointment policies;
- communication outside sessions;
- accessibility and travel time;
- privacy for telehealth sessions.

Insurance participation does not guarantee that every service will be covered. Verify network status and benefits with both the practice and your insurer.

## 7. Ask whether a brief consultation is available

Some therapists offer a short initial call before scheduling, while others use the first appointment to assess fit. A consultation is not a full evaluation or therapy session, and not every practice offers one.

If a brief call is available, use it to clarify the reason you are seeking support, the therapist's relevant experience, location and telehealth limits, availability, and practical questions. You do not need to disclose your full history during this first contact.

## 8. Revisit fit after therapy begins

Choosing a therapist is not a one-time test that you either pass or fail. As treatment develops, ask yourself:

- Do I understand what we are working toward?
- Can I raise disagreement or confusion?
- Does the therapist remember and respond to what matters to me?
- Are we reviewing progress rather than assuming it?
- Is the approach helping, or does the plan need to change?

The [American Psychological Association's guide to choosing a psychologist](https://www.apa.org/topics/psychotherapy/choose-therapist) encourages clients to consider both professional qualifications and the level of comfort and rapport they experience. If the work does not feel right, discuss the concern when it feels safe to do so. Sometimes that conversation strengthens therapy. Sometimes it confirms that a referral or a different provider is the better next step.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I ask a therapist before the first appointment?

Ask about licensure, experience with your concern, treatment approach, session format, language, availability, fees, insurance, confidentiality, and how progress will be discussed. You can also ask what happens if the therapist believes another provider would be a better fit.

### How can I verify an Illinois psychologist's license?

Use the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation license lookup and search the provider's name or license number. A directory profile or website can be helpful, but it is not a substitute for the official licensing record.

### Does a good personal connection matter more than credentials?

Both matter. Appropriate credentials, competence, and scope of practice are the starting requirements. Within that safe professional foundation, trust, collaboration, and agreement about goals and tasks can influence whether therapy feels workable and useful.

### How many sessions should I give a new therapist?

There is no universal number. Early sessions should help clarify goals, fit, and the proposed plan. If you feel misunderstood, unsafe, pressured, or unclear about the work, raise the concern when possible and consider another provider if it is not resolved.

### Can a therapist guarantee that treatment will work?

No ethical clinician can guarantee a particular result. A therapist should explain the proposed approach, its rationale, reasonable alternatives, limitations, and how progress will be reviewed.
