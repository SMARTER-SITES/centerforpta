Your surgeon has asked you to arrange a psychological evaluation before bariatric surgery. You may be wondering what you will be asked, how much of your history to discuss, or whether something you say could delay the operation.

Knowing what the appointment involves can make it easier to prepare. A bariatric psychological evaluation looks at your mental health, eating patterns, understanding of surgery, and the support you may need during recovery. The findings help your care team plan the next steps. [1.](https://asmbs.org/app/uploads/2022/10/ASMBS-IFSO-Guidelines-2022-PIIS1550728922006414.pdf)

## Why your surgical team asks for an evaluation

Preparing for surgery includes looking at anything that could make recovery or follow-up care difficult. This may involve current mental health symptoms, eating-related concerns, substance use, or practical problems at home. The assessment helps identify where treatment, information, or additional support may be needed. [1.](https://asmbs.org/app/uploads/2022/10/ASMBS-IFSO-Guidelines-2022-PIIS1550728922006414.pdf)

Before booking, ask your surgical program what it requires. Does it have a referral form? Does the evaluator need particular qualifications? Is there a deadline for the report? Getting those details early can prevent an appointment that does not meet your program’s requirements.

## What the psychologist may ask

The appointment usually includes a clinical interview. Topics may include changes in weight over time, eating habits, mood, previous mental health care, alcohol or drug use, and what you expect surgery to change. The clinician may also ask how you will manage appointments and recovery alongside work or family responsibilities. [2.](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/weight-loss/in-depth/gastric-bypass-surgery/art-20046318)

You can describe things in everyday language. If meals become irregular when you work late, explain that. If someone at home is worried about the operation, bring it up. Specific examples give the psychologist more to work with than answers you think are expected.

If a question feels uncomfortable, you can ask why it is relevant. You can also say that you need a moment, or that you are unsure how to answer. Be open about difficulties you are having, including anything you have not yet discussed with your surgical team.

## Will there be questionnaires or tests?

Questionnaires may be used alongside the interview and relevant records. They provide additional information about emotional functioning or eating-related concerns. The choice of measures depends on the clinical questions and the person being assessed; a questionnaire score is interpreted in context. [3.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27537008/)

Ask how much time to set aside and whether any forms need to be completed beforehand. If reading, language, or accessibility needs could affect the assessment, raise them when scheduling. The clinician can explain which arrangements are appropriate.

## What if I have a mental health history?

Having a history of depression, anxiety, or treatment does not by itself determine the recommendation. The psychologist considers how you are doing now, how symptoms affect daily life, and what care and support are in place. Some concerns may need attention before surgery can proceed. [4.](https://old.asmbs.org/app/uploads/2016/06/2016-Psych-Guidelines-published.pdf)

Bring a current medication list and mention any clinicians involved in your care. If you are worried that discussing a difficulty could affect the timing of surgery, say so.

## How to prepare

When you arrange the appointment, ask which documents the practice needs and how to send them securely. It may help to have the following ready:

- The referral, forms, or written requirements from your surgical program.

- The surgeon’s name, contact details, and any report deadline.

- A current medication list and details of relevant treatment, with records if requested.

- Questions you want answered about the appointment, report, fees, or next steps.

You do not need to rehearse your answers. A few notes about your history and the questions you want to ask are enough to help you remember what matters to you.

For example: “I understand the plan for surgery, but I am unsure how I will manage the first weeks at home.” Or: “I have had treatment for anxiety. What information would be useful for this evaluation?”

## The report and what happens next

The report summarizes relevant findings and recommendations. Depending on the assessment, these may include proceeding with the surgical process, arranging support, or addressing concerns before a further review. Ask the evaluator to explain any recommendation and what the next step involves. [4.](https://old.asmbs.org/app/uploads/2016/06/2016-Psych-Guidelines-published.pdf)

Before the assessment, clarify who will receive the report, what information it will contain, and how consent and confidentiality are handled. If your surgical program needs a particular form or wording, provide that requirement at the start.

The psychological evaluation contributes to the surgical team’s decision. It cannot guarantee that surgery will be offered, that an insurer will pay, or that a particular result will follow.

## Support before and after surgery

A pre-surgical evaluation gives your surgical team findings and recommendations to help plan your care. Ongoing counseling provides time to work on concerns such as body image, emotional eating, relationships, or adjustment after surgery. Read more about [bariatric counseling](https://centerforpta.com/bariatric-surgery-counseling/).

Follow-up continues after the operation. Your bariatric team will explain nutrition, supplements, activity, and medical reviews. If emotional difficulties arise during recovery, bring them to the team as well. [5.](https://asmbs.org/patients/life-after-bariatric-surgery/)

## Questions to ask before booking

How long will the evaluation take?

Ask about the appointment length, any questionnaires, and when to expect the report. The number of meetings and completion time can vary. If you have a surgical deadline, mention it before choosing a date.

Will my insurance cover it?

Confirm coverage for the specific evaluation with both the practice and your insurer. Ask about network status, any required authorization, and your expected share of the cost. Coverage for surgery and coverage for a psychological evaluation should be checked separately. You can start with the practice’s [rates and insurance information](https://centerforpta.com/rates-and-insurance/).

Can I have the appointment online?

An online format may be available when clinically appropriate and accepted by the referring program. For telehealth with Center for PTA, you must be physically located in Illinois during the appointment. Confirm the format and any testing requirements before booking.

Can a letter from my current therapist meet the requirement?

Ask your surgical program what it will accept. A treatment letter and a dedicated pre-surgical evaluation may serve different purposes. If you already have a therapist or psychiatrist, tell the evaluator so you can discuss whether information from that clinician would be useful.
